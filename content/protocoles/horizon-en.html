Measuring the dip of the sea horizon as a function of altitude

A high signal-to-noise test, feasible with a camera, a tray of liquid and a star, whose two possible outcomes are stated in advance.

Abstract

Horizon dip — the angle δ between the local horizontal, defined by the plumb line, and the sea horizon — is a geometric observable about which two models of the Earth's surface make incompatible, non-overlapping predictions. A spherical model predicts δ = √(2h/R′), growth as the square root of altitude; a plane model predicts δ = 0 at every altitude, the horizon being the vanishing line of the plane and therefore lying, by construction, at eye level.

The metrological value of this observable lies in the fact that atmospheric refraction — which dominates the uncertainty of most long-range geodetic measurements — enters only at second order: across the entire physically defensible range of the refraction coefficient (0 ≤ k ≤ 0.47), the spherical prediction varies only from 107′ to 78′ at 3 107 m elevation. The gap between the two models is therefore at least 78 arcminutes, against an estimated instrumental error budget of 2.2′ (1 σ) — a signal-to-noise ratio of order 36 against the plane hypothesis, and of 29 on the determination of the coefficient a, where refraction uncertainty enters in full. The two ratios are distinguished in section 09.

We propose a distributed campaign: measure δ from a range of altitudes spanning a few metres to a few kilometres, and test not an isolated point but the law δ(′) = a·√h(m). The spherical model requires 1.40 ≤ a ≤ 1.93 depending on refraction; the plane model requires a = 0. Decision criteria are fixed in section 08, prior to any acquisition.

01Purpose

This document invites independent observers to carry out a simple angular measurement whose result discriminates between two geometric models of the Earth's surface, and to publish their raw data whatever the outcome.

The choice of observable is not arbitrary. Most observations invoked in this debate — visibility of distant objects, long-range photography, levelling of water surfaces — share a common weakness: their interpretation depends on a poorly constrained atmospheric parameter, the refraction coefficient k, whose real variability is sufficient to span the gap between the models. The argument then shifts onto the value of k and never concludes.

Horizon dip escapes this trap for a structural reason: it varies as √(1−k), that is, very slowly, whereas hidden-height measurements vary as 1/(1−k) and as the square of distance. Multiplying k fourfold reduces δ by only 27 per cent. No value of k brings a sphere to δ = 0.

02Theoretical basis

For an observer whose eye is at height h above a spherical surface of radius R, the grazing line of sight touches the surface at the point of tangency. The angle between that line and the local horizontal plane is, to first order in h/R:

δ = √( 2h / R′ ),   R′ = R / (1 − k) δ in radians, h and R′ in metres. k = terrestrial refraction coefficient.

Expressed in arcminutes with h in metres, the relation becomes a single-parameter square root law:

δ(′) = a · √h(m),   a = 3 437.75 · √( 2(1−k) / R ) The coefficient a is the only quantity to be measured. It is the slope of δ against √h.

On a plane surface, the horizon is the vanishing line of the surface plane. In perspective projection a vanishing line lies exactly in the plane through the eye parallel to the receding plane — here, at eye level. The prediction is therefore δ = 0, independent of h: this is a property of projective geometry, not an approximation.

If refraction is granted to the plane model

The objection is legitimate and must be answered: the spherical prediction includes refraction, the plane's is allowed only straight-line propagation. The comparison looks unbalanced.

It is not, and the imbalance favours the plane model. Ray curvature is 1/ρ with k = R/ρ: it depends only on the atmosphere, and is therefore the same above a plane. The relative curvature of ray and surface then becomes:

sphere: 1/R − 1/ρ = (1−k)/R   |   plane: 0 − 1/ρ = −k/R Positive on a sphere while k < 1: a horizon exists. Negative on a plane for any k.

On a plane with a real atmosphere the ray therefore curves more than the surface: the ground rises around the observer instead of falling away. From 3 107 m, sighting 1° above the horizontal, the ground would be met at 1 873 km; at 5°, at 8 589 km. No horizon would exist at all and the ground would fill the sky.

What this establishes

δ = 0 is the plane model's best case, obtained by granting it straight-line propagation. Granting it refraction does not improve matters: it leads to an absurd and massively refuted prediction. This is also why no intermediate value is available: the plane model has no mechanism producing a dip of a few tens of arcminutes.

Exact form

The exact relation is δ = arccos( R′ / (R′ + h) ), of which √(2h/R′) is the first-order expansion. The difference is 0.018′ at 3 107 m and 0.036′ at 5 000 m — below the error budget, but the exact form costs nothing and is the one to use in reduction.

04What this protocol does not invent

Added in version 1.2, rewritten in 1.7. A document that announces its own precedents is stronger than one that leaves the reader to find them — provided it applies to them the standard it imposes on itself.

The relation δ = √(2h/R′) is not this document's construction: it is the dip correction used in celestial navigation, tabulated in Bowditch and in every nautical almanac. Every sextant sight applies it.

Precision added in 1.7: earlier versions wrote “since the nineteenth century”. That referred to the Bowditch tabulation, whose first edition dates from 1802. The correction itself is older — it appears in navigation authors from the seventeenth century — and the sextant dates from 1731. The formula is therefore more than two centuries old, and the earlier phrasing could be read as saying it is exactly two.

The double standard, and its correction

What version 1.2 did without noticing

This protocol asks, throughout, that one not take a table on trust: measure for yourself, publish the raw files, fix the criteria before acquisition, measure uncertainties instead of postulating them.

Two things that require trusting no one

The Bowditch coefficient. Open an almanac, read 0.97 per root foot, convert, compare with our table 1. It is an internal check: no one else's data enter.

The consequence for a three-body fix. An altitude error common to all three sights displaces each line by the same amount and opens the cocked hat — for an error e, the side is 2e√3. Ignoring dip from a 15 m bridge, where it is 7.0′, would therefore open a triangle some twenty nautical miles across, where practice closes one or two. That is a geometric consequence of the intercept method, which anyone can recompute.

These two are of a different kind from “such a team found this”, and the document no longer mixes them.

What that changes, and what it does not

The formula is old and massively used. This protocol does not pose an open question, and does not claim to.

What it brings is something else: not “nobody has measured this”, but nobody has measured it to this standard — timestamped pre-registration, criteria fixed in advance, uncertainties measured rather than postulated, raw files published whatever the outcome. The table above shows that place to be vacant. That is this document's only claim, and it stands independently of what others found.

Two of these precedents finally deserve mention for what they cost our side, and it would be dishonest to pass over them. Wallace 1870 is precisely the argument of our section 02 — granting refraction to the plane model does not help it — formulated a hundred and fifty years before us. And Rainy Lake is the experiment whose methodology our own site praises, and whose announced result agrees with the spherical model. We have not examined its data; we therefore do not invoke it as proof, and neither do we conceal it.

05Order of operations

Added in version 1.5. Every necessary element was already present in the sections that follow, but scattered across six of them and without their order. And the order is not indifferent: several steps make the preceding ones unrecoverable. This section does not repeat the procedures — it says in what order to take them, and which one cannot be redone.

The five things that can never be recovered afterwards
  1. The timestamp. Deposited after the first frame, it proves nothing. Nothing that follows has evidential value without it.
  2. Cartographic checking of the azimuth. A distant coastline in the sightline is indistinguishable from the horizon in the photograph. The reading is lost and it looks sound.
  3. The elevation above the sea at the time. Below 50 m it requires an on-site measurement. Home again with a GPS height, you no longer have a station.
  4. ΔT, tide and sea state. Recorded at the moment of exposure, or never. Tide can be recovered from tables; sea state and water temperature cannot.
  5. Taping the focus ring. It must precede the measurement frame. If focus shifts between that frame and the solving frame, the scale no longer carries across and the measurement has no unit.

Phase 0 — before the first frame, once for the whole campaign

  1. Deposit the protocolFreeze the PDF, compute its SHA-256 hash, deposit the file on OSF or Zenodo, obtain the DOI, publish DOI and hash. Section 13. Until that is done, photograph nothing.
  2. Fix the pointing criterionThe one for the horizon line, stated in section 07, and circulate it to every observer. It matters less that the criterion be the best than that it be the same everywhere: a bias common to all shows up in the fitted intercept, a bias that varies between observers shows up nowhere.
  3. Choose the spread of stationsAt least one above 300 m — below that the test is not decisive, as table 11 quantifies. Spread the others evenly in √h, not in h: √h is the fitting variable.
  4. Draw the orderIf the fallback is used, the normal/inverted alternation is drawn before the session, not during it.

Phase 1 — preparing the station, several days ahead

  1. Check the azimuth on a chartOpen sea over the whole theoretical range for the altitude — 213 km from 3 107 m, 12 km from 10 m. No coastline, no island, no shoal in the sightline. Irreversible step: neglected, it yields a normal-looking reading of no value.
  2. Establish the eye's elevationAbove the sea surface at the time, orthometric. Not the GPS ellipsoidal height. Below 50 m, plan a direct on-site measurement to the decimetre. Section 09.
  3. Locate the windowEphemerides: solar altitude, target, its azimuth and true altitude. Venus or Jupiter in preference to a star — they can be acquired well before nightfall, when the sea horizon is still fully legible. Section 07.
  4. Get the tide timeFor the chosen slot, and if possible prefer a low-range moment: at 5 m elevation, two metres of tide are worth 41 per cent of δ.

Phase 2 — on site, before the frames. Order matters

  1. Safety firstThis protocol sends people onto cliffs and summits, at night. Scout the ground by day, do not work alone at the edge, and plan for red light and cold. No measurement is worth an accident.
  2. Set up the tripod and the opticsFocus at infinity, intermediate aperture. Then tape the focus ring and do not touch it again until the night is over — the solving frame depends on it.
  3. Set up the trayOff the tripod, on sand or foam, sheltered from wind, viscous liquid, read at the centre. Then let it settle: it is the only moment of the evening when waiting is productive.
  4. Record the conditionsAir temperature at eye height, sea surface temperature, ΔT, pressure, humidity, wind, sea state and significant wave height. Irreversible step.
  5. Rehearse the framingTarget, reflection and horizon line in the field, before the window opens and not during it. The window lasts twenty to thirty minutes; it is not the moment to discover that two degrees of field are missing.

Phase 3 — acquisition

  1. The burstTwenty successive frames on the same sightline. Raw file, uncropped. An isolated frame has no estimable uncertainty and does not count as a measurement.
  2. RepeatThree series minimum, at distinct hours, to sample refraction variability.
  3. Cross the methodsIf both rigs are available: one series with the artificial horizon and one with the inclinometer and reversal, same site, same hour. This cannot be made up on another date.
  4. The solving frameIn full darkness, same setting, ring still taped: a populated star field, meant solely for astrometry. It is the last frame of the evening, and forgetting it costs the whole evening.

Phase 4 — reduction, at the desk, in this order

  1. Solve the fieldScale in arcseconds per pixel, residuals, distortion profile. Everything else is expressed in that unit: this is where to start.
  2. Pass the free checkTarget-to-reflection separation divided by two, compared with the ephemeris altitude plus refraction. If they do not agree to 0.5 per cent, stop and find out why before reducing anything. Section 07.
  3. Point the horizonBy the criterion fixed in phase 0, without exception, recording the column-to-column standard deviation and the number of columns discarded.
  4. Reduce δThe midpoint between target and reflection gives the true horizontal; the offset between that midpoint and the horizon line is δ.
  5. Measure the uncertainties, do not postulate themPointing σ = scatter of the horizon position over the burst. Surface σ = scatter of the reflection over the same burst, divided by two.
  6. Correct the elevationFor the actual tide at the moment of exposure, not the mean tide.
  7. SubmitThe full record sheet of section 12 and the raw files, whatever the result. A station is discarded only on a written and published ground; never because the number displeases.

Phase 5 — campaign, once every station is in

  1. Linear fitWeighted regression of δ against √h. Slope a, intercept b, and confidence intervals on both.
  2. Free-exponent fitlnδ against ln h. The exponent p must come out at 0.500, and its error bar says so.
  3. PublishRaw data, fits, and the conclusion they impose — including the stations in anomalous refractive regime, kept and flagged.

What to avoid absolutely

Two lists, and the distinction between them is worth keeping: the first destroys the measurement without leaving a trace, the second degrades it visibly.

WHAT DESTROYS THE MEASUREMENT

And does not show afterwards

Cropping the file — the optical centre is lost, and with it the reference.
Letting the raw converter correct the lens or lift contrast — the geometry changes.
Refocusing between the measurement frame and the solving frame — the scale no longer carries.
Taking the elevation from GPS without orthometric conversion — 178 per cent error at 10 m for a 30 m offset.
A coastline, an island or a haze bank in the sightline — that is not the geometric horizon.
A single frame — no estimable uncertainty.
Discarding a station because the number displeases.

WHAT DEGRADES THE MEASUREMENT

Water in the tray at an exposed site — use a viscous liquid.
The tray on the tripod — it passes on its vibration.
Reading near the edge of the tray — the meniscus curves the surface.

An internal-focus lens, whose focal length varies with distance.
Working at one altitude only — it is a law being tested, not a point.
Confusing a level horizon in the frame with a level optical axis.

06Equipment

Reorganised in version 1.4. Earlier versions gave one list, inherited from 1.0 and still built around the inclinometer: it mentioned neither tray, nor liquid, nor ephemerides. Anyone following it could not carry out method A — which has been the reference procedure since 1.1.

Common to both methods

  • Camera with sensor of known dimensions, a prime or lockable lens, and a stable tripod. See the table below for choosing the focal length.
  • Unobstructed sea horizon over at least the theoretical range for the altitude in question — 213 km from 3 107 m, 12 km from 10 m.
  • Barometer and thermometer, to document conditions.
  • A second thermometer, for sea surface temperature — a probe on a line, or an infrared thermometer aimed at the water. It is the air-to-water difference that governs the thermal gradient of the boundary layer, and therefore k at low stations. See section 10.
  • An elevation referred to the sea surface at the time, not to the ellipsoid. This is the protocol's most dangerous point at low stations; see section 09.
  • Tide state and sea state at the moment of acquisition.
  • An ephemeris application — Stellarium, Sky Safari, or the IMCCE tables. It serves three purposes: choosing the target, knowing its true altitude to the arcsecond, and locating the twilight window.
  • Plate-solving software — Astrometry.net online, ASTAP or SIRIL offline. It supplies the angular scale; see section 09.

For method A — artificial horizon, the reference procedure

  • A wide, shallow tray. Thirty centimetres across is enough; what matters is the surface, not the volume.
  • A viscous liquid — liquid paraffin or glycerine, a few decilitres. See the procedure for the reason.
  • Something to shelter the tray from wind, and something to stand it on away from the tripod: a box of sand, a slab of foam.
  • Optional — a flat glass cover, if wind demands it, with the 180° rotation procedure.

For the fallback — inclinometer

  • An inclinometer. A digital construction level (±0.05°) suffices; a phone inclinometer (±0.5°) is acceptable provided reversal is applied.
  • A tripod allowing inverted mounting: flip head, L-bracket, or reversed centre column.

07Procedure — Method A, artificial horizon

This method is new in version 1.1 and replaces levelling as the reference procedure. It requires neither a levelling instrument nor a bias calibration: the horizontal reference makes itself. One quantity remains to be established, the angular scale, and section 09 says how — the “no calibration” of earlier versions was one shortcut too many.

What the protocol assumes here, and what it does not

The protocol advances no hypothesis about the cause of the perpendicularity between a surface at rest and the plumb line. It uses only the fact, checkable with a set square by anyone, that it holds. The plumb line is an instrument here, not a theory: it is the material reference to which δ is referred, and the reasoning that follows is purely geometric.

A still water surface sets itself perpendicular to the plumb line. A star and its reflection are therefore symmetric about the true horizontal, and their midpoint materialises it. If the sea horizon line appears in the same frame, δ is read directly.

  1. Set up the surfaceA wide, shallow tray, sheltered from wind. Use a viscous liquid rather than water: liquid paraffin (≈ 70 mm²/s) and glycerine (≈ 1 100 mm²/s) damp capillary ripples tens to a thousand times faster than water (1 mm²/s). It is the only remedy for high-frequency vibration when a high-altitude site offers no genuinely calm shelter — and at 300 or 3 000 m that is the rule rather than the exception. Stand the tray on sand or foam, never on the tripod, which passes on its own vibration. Read at the centre of the tray, not near the edges where the meniscus curves the surface. Let it settle.
  2. If wind forces a glass coverGlass never has its two faces rigorously parallel, and that residual prism deflects the ray. Take a second frame with the cover turned through 180° and average the two: the prism cancels exactly, by the same reasoning as camera reversal.
  3. Choose the targetBetween 3° and 5° altitude, in the azimuth of the horizon under study. The vertical field required is 2a, that is 6° to 10°; a 100 mm lens offers 13.7°. Aim for 3° rather than 5°: at 5° the margin is only 1.85° on each side, and it must still absorb the dip, which reaches 1.7° at 3 107 m. Version 1.3 wrote here that a 100 mm was “ample”: true at 3°, only just at 5°.
  4. Frame all threeStar, reflection and horizon line in the same image. Focus at infinity: all three lie there, the reflection included.
  5. ReduceThe midpoint between star and reflection gives the true horizontal. The offset between that midpoint and the horizon line is δ.
  6. RepeatThree series minimum, at distinct hours.
  7. Measure turbulence instead of assuming itA burst of twenty successive frames on the same sightline. The standard deviation of the horizon position across those twenty frames IS the pointing term of the error budget. A grazing sightline crosses hundreds of kilometres of air near the surface — the geometry that produces the most optical turbulence — and a postulated figure would be optimistic there.
  8. Measure the surface's unrest instead of assuming itOn the same burst of twenty frames, take the scatter of the reflection's position. A tilt ε of the surface displaces the reflection by 2ε and their midpoint by ε: the scatter of the reflection divided by two is the surface's contribution to δ. It is measured, not postulated. Beyond 1′, change liquid or shelter before going on.
  9. Cross the two methodsOne series with the artificial horizon AND one with the inclinometer and reversal, same site, same hour. The symmetry of the artificial horizon is an assumption until it is tested: the direct and reflected paths do not cross rigorously the same low layers. The gap between the two methods bounds that assumption.
Why refraction does not interfere

The direct image of the star and its reflected image undergo the same refraction and remain symmetric about the horizontal. Their midpoint is therefore free of refraction, even though each of the two images is affected by it.

When to shoot — the observing window, added in 1.4

This is method A's heaviest practical constraint, and no earlier version mentioned it: the frame must hold the target, its reflection and the sea horizon line at once. In full darkness the sea horizon line is no longer visible.

The problem is old and already solved. It is exactly that of the sextant star sight, which celestial navigation takes at nautical twilight for this precise reason: the star has appeared, the horizon has not yet gone. The protocol therefore inherits the solution along with the formula (section 04).

Consequence for plate solving

In that window the field holds only a handful of stars — sometimes too few for a solver, which wants about a dozen. Take the measurement frame in the window, then a second frame in full darkness, same setting, ring taped, meant only for the solve. It is the scale that carries from one frame to the other, and it depends only on the optical setting — provided, precisely, that nothing was touched.

Where exactly the horizon line is — added in 1.4

The horizon line is not a stroke: it is an intensity step spread over several pixels. At 100 mm on 4 000 pixels of height, one pixel is 0.21′ and three pixels 0.62′ — a quarter of the instrumental budget. Two operators pointing “by eye” are therefore not measuring the same quantity, and the discrepancy shows up nowhere in the data.

The criterion, to be fixed before reduction and applied without exception:

  1. Work on the raw, uncropped file, with no automatic lens correction and no contrast enhancement.
  2. For each pixel column, take the vertical intensity profile around the transition and read the ordinate of the half-height point of the step, by sub-pixel interpolation between the sea level and the sky level.
  3. Average over the full usable width, after discarding columns containing a ship, an islet or a haze bank — and record how many columns were discarded.
  4. Record the column-to-column standard deviation of that mean: it measures the regularity of the line, and an anomalous value flags a disturbed refractive regime before one even looks at the result.

There is nothing sacred about the half-height criterion. What matters is that it be the same at every station: a bias common to all shows up in the fitted intercept, a bias that varies between observers shows up nowhere. Any other reproducible criterion will do, provided it is stated in advance and applied throughout.

A free check on the same frame — added in 1.4

The angular separation between the target and its reflection is exactly twice its apparent altitude. And the ephemeris gives its true altitude to the arcsecond. The comparison costs nothing and is worth making.

measured separation ÷ 2 = true altitude + refraction At 3° altitude refraction is about 14.3′; at 4°, 11.7′; at 5°, 9.9′.

What this check establishes. It verifies the angular scale to about 0.5 per cent, independently of the solver — two routes that agree are worth more than one taken on trust. And it flags an atmosphere in an unusual state if the deduced refraction departs markedly from the standard value for the recorded pressure and temperature.

What it does not establish. It does not measure k along the grazing sightline. The path to a target at 3° crosses the whole troposphere obliquely, whereas the horizon line grazes the boundary layer over tens of kilometres: these are two distinct quantities, and conflating them would be exactly the kind of shortcut this document reproaches elsewhere.

Fallback — levelling and reversal

If an artificial horizon is impractical, measure with an inclinometer by the procedure below. It remains valid, but it depends on the repeatability of the station setup, where the artificial horizon does not.

  1. Set upTripod on stable ground, base levelled in both axes. Pitch is the critical quantity; roll affects only framing.
  2. Acquire the horizon, normal positionFocus at infinity, mid aperture, fast shutter. Save the raw file, uncropped.
  3. Acquire the horizon, inverted positionRemount the camera upside down on the same base, re-level, reproduce the same framing.
  4. Angular scalePlate-solve a star field taken at the same setting, ring taped — section 09. Do not trust the engraved focal length, nominal to 2–5 per cent, nor the lunar standard, at 7 per cent. Version 1.3 added section 09 without correcting this step, which still recommended the focal length: an internal contradiction, lifted in 1.4.
  5. ReductionMeasure in pixels the ordinate of the file centre and of the horizon line. Convert to angle using the sensor's vertical field of view.
  6. RepetitionAt least three series at distinct times, to sample refraction variability.

Eliminating mounting bias by reversal

The optical axis is generally not rigorously parallel to the camera base, and every inclinometer carries a zero error. These two defects combine into a pitch bias e undetectable in a single acquisition. In the inverted position e changes sign; the mean of the two readings eliminates it exactly.

δ = ( δnormal + δinverted ) / 2 A discrepancy greater than 20′ between the two readings indicates a large mounting bias — the mean remains valid, but repeatability must be checked.

08Procedure — Method B, differential measurement

This variant requires neither a levelling instrument nor a bias calibration — the angular scale is still needed, as everywhere else. It consists of photographing the same horizon from two widely separated altitudes, with identical equipment and settings, and measuring the displacement of the horizon line within the frame. Any mounting bias, being identical in both acquisitions, cancels by difference.

Restriction added in version 1.1

Cancellation of the bias assumes it is identical in both acquisitions. But going from 10 m to 3 107 m requires dismounting the camera, travelling, and remounting: the bias is no longer the same. A remounting repeatability of 20′ consumes 21 per cent of the 94′ differential signal.

Method B remains useful when the camera-head assembly is not dismounted between the two stations. Otherwise use the artificial horizon of Method A, which depends on no mounting repeatability at all.

Earlier versions announced a single figure, and not always the same one: 36 in the abstract, 23 here, 45 in the altitude table. Three numbers on one page. They in fact answer two distinct questions, and conflating them let an error pass for a correction.

Question 1 — is the surface flat? The signal is the gap between δ = 0 and the smallest dip a sphere can produce, that is 78.2′ at 3 107 m, reached at k = 0.47. The noise is the instrumental budget, 2.2′. Ignorance of k does not enter: it is already absorbed by taking the least favourable case, and no value of k brings a sphere closer to zero. Ratio: 36.

A term version 1.2 left unquantified

δ is read as an offset in pixels, converted to an angle by the focal length and the pixel pitch. But the focal length engraved on a lens is nominal: the true focal length at infinity commonly departs from it by 2 to 5 per cent, and focus breathing varies it further. A relative error on the scale carries over in full to δ. Version 1.2 set aside the lunar standard — ±7 per cent of the diameter, that is 7.0′ — in favour of the “sensor method”, without noticing that the latter carries a term of its own.

The clean solution exists and it is free: field astrometry, or plate solving.

  1. On the frame itself, or on one taken at the same setting without touching the ring, solve the star field — Astrometry.net, ASTAP, SIRIL, PixInsight, all free or open.
  2. The solver returns the scale in arcseconds per pixel, fitted on dozens of catalogue stars, with its residuals. Ordinary accuracy is of order 0.05 per cent — that is 0.05′ in 100′, a thousand times better than the nominal focal length and a hundred times better than the lunar standard.
  3. It also returns the field's distortion profile (SIP terms), which disposes of the “optical distortion” line of the error table at the same stroke.

Method A already puts a star and its reflection in the field: the acquisition is nocturnal, the field is populated, the solve is immediate. For a daytime frame, photograph a star field the same evening at the identical setting, ring taped. The solved scale and the solver's residuals become mandatory fields on the record sheet: an angular measurement whose scale is undocumented has no estimable uncertainty.

Decision criteria, fixed a priori

δ MEASURED ∈ [ 0 ; 20′ ] AT 3 107 m

Hypothesis S refuted

No value of k within the physical range falls below 78′. A measurement in this interval, reproduced over three series, refutes the spherical model by direct measurement.

δ MEASURED ∈ [ 78′ ; 107′ ] AT 3 107 m

Hypothesis P refuted

The exact position within the interval additionally provides an independent estimate of the refraction coefficient at the time of observation.

Dead zone — 20′ to 78′

Neither hypothesis predicts this range. A result falling within it must be treated as a protocol failure, not a physical result. Causes to rule out, in order of frequency: faulty levelling in pitch, false horizon line (coastline, island, haze bank), cropping of the file, focal length changed between acquisitions.

10Known systematics and failure modes

  1. False horizon line. The most frequent and most insidious failure mode. A distant coastline, an island, a mountain range or a haze bank produces a visual limit that is not the geometric horizon. Cartographic verification before occupying the station is mandatory.
  2. Pitch confused with roll. A horizon rendered level in the image says nothing about the vertical tilt of the optical axis, the only quantity measured here.
  3. Cropping. Any cropped image loses the position of its optical centre and therefore its horizontal reference. Work exclusively on the native file.
  4. Optical distortion. Wide-angle lenses curve the horizon off axis. Place the horizon line near the centre, or use a telephoto lens.
  5. Focal drift. Focal length must be identical between the measurement frame and the frame meant for plate solving — the whole validity of carrying the scale across rests on it. Tape the ring, and beware internal-focus lenses, whose focal length varies with distance.
  6. Anomalous refraction regime. A horizon that undulates, doubles, or shows a sharp cut-off line indicates an atmospheric duct. The measurement remains valid but the value of k it implies is not transferable to other observations. Always record the appearance of the horizon.
  7. Single measurement. An isolated acquisition has no estimable uncertainty. Three series are the minimum.

These values assume the gradient holds along the whole sightline. In practice the affected layer is only metres thick — which is exactly why it dominates below 50 m, where the sightline never leaves it, and becomes negligible from a summit. It also accounts for disagreement between two readings at the same site at different hours, where nothing instrumental has changed.

What this imposes on the record sheet

Record the sea surface temperature and the air temperature at eye height, and log their difference ΔT = Tair − Tsea. A reading without ΔT does not characterise its own regime.

ΔT > +2 °C (colder water): stable layer, inversion, duct possible. The horizon looks distant and too sharp, and δ is smaller than the standard prediction.
ΔT < −1 °C (warmer water): unstable layer, inferior mirage. The horizon looks close and boiling, and δ is larger.
|ΔT| < 1 °C: near-standard regime. These are the most useful stations, and they should be sought rather than awaited.

Stations at |ΔT| > 2 °C are kept, published and flagged, then analysed separately. None is deleted: that is the rule set in section 10, and it admits no exception.

The direction of these effects is worth stressing, because it runs against common intuition: it is cold air over warm water that brings the horizon closer and deepens the dip, and warm air over cold water that pushes it away, to the point of raising objects reputed to be hidden. A spectacularly long-range observation is therefore not an observation in the ordinary regime, and ΔT says so.

11Distributed campaign — what we propose

The value of a collective campaign goes beyond repeating a single measurement. Plotting δ against √h turns the question into three independent constraints, each separately verifiable.

  1. The slope. The fitted coefficient a must fall within [1.40; 1.93] for hypothesis S, or be consistent with zero for hypothesis P.
  2. Linearity. Points must align in δ against √h. A relation of any other form would refute both hypotheses and indicate an unidentified systematic.
  3. Passage through the origin. The fitted intercept must be consistent with zero. A significantly non-zero value would reveal a bias common to all observers.

We therefore invite observers at staggered altitudes — from sea level to high-altitude stations — to contribute one point each. About ten points spread over the range 5 m – 3 000 m suffice to constrain the slope to better than 5 per cent.

Fitting method

Version 1.1 stated the three constraints without saying how to test them. They are tested thus:

  • Weighted regression of δ against √h, weights being the inverse square of each station's uncertainty — the signal-to-noise table by altitude already supplies them. Unweighted, a station at 5 m counts as much as one at 3 000 m, which is absurd.
  • Confidence intervals on the slope a AND on the intercept, not just points. A slope of 1.7 with no error bar says nothing.
  • Randomised order: alternate normal and inverted in a drawn order, and vary azimuths. Without this, a slow refraction drift through the session is indistinguishable from an instrumental bias.

The free exponent — added in version 1.3

The three constraints above assume the form δ = a√h. That grants in advance what the measurement ought to establish. The general form δ = a·hp, with the exponent p left free, tests it instead of postulating it.

  • The sphere predicts p = 0.500 exactly, with no adjustable parameter.
  • The plane predicts a = 0, and p then loses all meaning.
  • A law linear in h — what one would get if δ were in fact measuring a bias proportional to the height of the mounting — would give p = 1.

The fit is carried out in logarithms, lnδ = ln a + p ln h, weighted by (δ/σδ)². With six stations staggered from 10 m to 3 107 m, one series each and the error budget of section 09:

What the exponent can settle

σp = 0.022, and 0.013 with three series per station. The gap between p = 0.5 and p = 1 is therefore 22 σ; even p = 0.6 would be separated from p = 0.5 at 4.5 σ. The exponent is measurable. It need not be assumed, and assuming it was the last concession this protocol made to the hypothesis it claims to test.

Two precautions. First, the logarithmic fit forces b = 0: the linear fit δ = a√h + b must therefore be run as well, and b checked for consistency with zero. If b is not consistent with zero, the exponent drawn from the logarithm is contaminated by that bias, and both fits must be redone together, on three parameters.

Second, k varying with altitude — the sensitivity table — biases the exponent upward. If low stations work at k = 0.15 and the summit at k = 0.10, δ is 2.9 per cent larger there, which adds +0.005 to p. Five times under the statistical uncertainty, but to be logged rather than discovered afterwards.

13Commitment

This protocol was written before any data were acquired, and states in advance the meaning of each of the two outcomes. That is the condition of its evidential value.

What is still missing, and must be done before collecting

A version number proves nothing. “Version 1.7” is not opposable to a sceptical reader: nothing materially prevents rewriting a document after seeing the data.

DOI of this deposit: DOI-PENDING While this line reads “pending”, the pre-registration is not binding, and the document says so itself.

A timestamp verifiable by a third party is required. The procedure, revised in version 1.6 so that the DOI appears in the document itself rather than beside it:

  1. Open the deposit without publishing it. On Zenodo, create the record and request Reserve DOI: the DOI is assigned before publication. OSF offers the same.
  2. Write the reserved DOI into the two slots provided — front page and above — then regenerate the French and English PDFs.
  3. Freeze. No modification afterwards, not even a typo: any retouching invalidates the hashes.
  4. Compute the SHA-256 hash of each of the two frozen files.
  5. Upload both files to the SAME record — the DOI bears on the record, not on each file — paste the hashes into the notes field, and publish.
  6. Only then begin collecting.
Why the DOI can sit inside the file and the hash cannot

The DOI is assigned before publication: it can therefore enter the document, which is then frozen and hashed. The hash, by contrast, is computed on the finished file — writing it in would alter the file, and so falsify it. It necessarily lives outside: in the record's notes, and wherever the protocol is announced.

The DOI supplies the date, the hash supplies the file's exact identity; either alone can be worked around, the two together make the pre-registration binding.

A point of method, to forestall a legitimate objection. Writing in the DOI does not change the version number: it fills a slot opened for the purpose. It follows that version 1.6 exists publicly only in its form bearing the DOI. The copy with an empty slot is an intermediate working state and must not circulate: two distinct files claiming the same version number would be exactly the kind of ambiguity this arrangement exists to remove.

We undertake to publish the entirety of the data received, including those contradicting the hypothesis we judged more probable, and to publish the conclusion the data impose. A protocol whose result were known in advance would not be worth executing; this one is proposed precisely because its outcome is not.

Methodological comments, corrections and proposals for improving the protocol are invited before the campaign begins, and will be incorporated into a credited version 2.
